Useful Nonsense Part 3: Sitting in the Song You Can’t Skip

On present-moment awareness, and why you get whatever is playing.

October 17, 2026 · Brian Mulligan, LPCC, CAT

Anyone who has religiously watched The Big Lebowski like me knows that The Dude hates the fuckin’ Eagles, man. So much so that when he gets into a spat with his cab driver about the band, he gets dumped somewhere in the middle of Los Angeles and has to walk his way home.

The Dude (Usually) Abides

The funny part is that The Dude is usually excellent at listening to whatever song is playing. He finds joy in life’s simple pleasures (a White Russian, bowling, a nice rug), and wherever he is, he’s mostly just there. One thing at a time. The lane in front of him, the drink in his hand. But when he finds himself embroiled in a kidnapping case and pushed into the role of amateur P.I., his attention goes everywhere except where his feet are. He becomes, in a word, “very un-Dude.”

Part of the hilarity of the film is seeing our Jeffrey Lebowski flail under pressure, taking proverbial pratfalls and stepping on rakes. It’s a delightful kind of schadenfreude to watch him drop a joint in his crotch and plow his already-besieged car into a trashcan. But the real magic of the film is that we are rooting for this lazy, lovable, unemployed everyman. We want him back in his lane, back in the moment, back to bowling and candle-lit baths. Seeing The Dude in his element speaks to an inner Dude in all of us.

Seeing The Dude in his element speaks to an inner Dude in all of us.

Sometimes the Song Sucks

When pressures mount, it’s normal to experience stress. None of us are immune to it. Yet so many of us reflexively respond to stress by trying to escape it, and when we can’t leave the car, the mind leaves anyway. One of its first lines of defense against unpleasantness is to attempt to go somewhere else.

When we can’t leave the car, the mind leaves anyway.

When the Eagles come on, The Dude has two options. He can let the song play, or he can fight with the radio (and the cabbie). He fights, and it costs him his ride. Part 2 covered what to do with an unwelcome feeling (stop swimming against the current). Now, faithful reader, we’re looking at something much sneakier: where your attention goes while the feeling is happening.

Where the Mind Goes When You Can’t Leave

I like to imagine The Dude in that backseat. He’s been bamboozled. He’s been drugged. He’s experienced a particularly frightening flashback and had a coffee cup chucked at his head. Life is hard to make sense of sometimes, and The Dude was doing his best to try to put the disjointed pieces together – trying to figure it all out. This is not the Jeffrey Lebowski we know and love – it is a very un-Dude way of being. Physically, he’s in the cab, but mentally he’s somewhere else entirely, reviewing the past or trying to predict the future.

Research has a name for the brain circuitry involved in this “elsewhere” state of mind: the default mode network. It is most active when you aren’t occupied with anything in front of you, and it fills the downtime with the past, the future, and the ongoing story of you. Your puppet master, it turns out, operates a time machine, and this is the engine room. It only travels to two destinations: the thing that already went wrong and the thing that might.

Nobody Plays Music to Reach the Last Note

Alan Watts – a cool and smart guy largely credited with popularizing mindfulness in “The West” – had a way of describing this. We treat life like a trip, he said, with a destination up ahead where the real thing finally starts. Music works differently. The point isn’t to play a song with the objective to get to the end. If arrival were the point, musicians would write songs with only one note, and the best orchestras would be the ones that finished fastest. The playing is the point.

The playing is the point.

The Fallacy of Finally Arriving

Some people call it the arrival fallacy: the belief that somewhere past this project, this quarter, this divorce, or this Friday, your real life finally begins. Every finish line turns into the next starting block. The promotion lands and “once this is done” relocates to the next quarter. The deal closes and a new one opens. It’s a treadmill with the speed button stuck, and the man on it keeps checking the display to see how far he’s gone.

Every finish line turns into the next starting block.

If you’ve hit every mark and still feel behind, this is a big part of why. The marks keep moving because the lane you’re standing in is always the one you’re hurrying to leave.

But I Can’t Just Sit Still and Do Nothing

Though many of us appreciate The Dude’s philosophy, few of us actually live it. In other words, stalwart reader, it’s more likely than not that you and I have qualities that The Dude doesn’t, namely ambition. So, does “being present” mean you can never plan anything? Does one have to live without goals, discard progress, and abandon intention? One does not.

Present moment awareness is flexibility of attention. You can visit the future on purpose, handle your business, and return. The trouble starts when the trip has no return ticket and the puppet master is driving the time machine. You can be fully in the moment and furious, or fully in the moment and scared. Calm may or may not tag along. What gets trained is where your attention sits.

How Being Dumb Works for The Dude – and You Can, Too

Your reactive mind that thinks it’s so smart and clever wants to diagnose why you can’t focus and engineer a fix. But there’s a part of you – the dumb part – that requires no such convolutions.

Try this: pick a song you actively dislike (Eagles optional). Play it all the way through, with no skipping, no volume knob, and no phone in your other hand. Put your attention on one plain thing: the song itself, the weight of your feet, or the sound underneath the other sounds. Your mind will likely leave within a few seconds. By the second chorus it will be drafting tomorrow’s to-do list and arguing with the lyrics. Each departure is the mind leaving the cab, and each return is one rep. Bringing it back is the exercise.

Bringing it back is the exercise.

The Dude Abides

Part 2 said you can’t make the floor foam. Part 3 adds that you can’t skip the song. It is the song of your life, and in each moment, you get whatever is playing.

There is wisdom our blundering man in his bathrobe can impart. He is, in his typical resting state, present-moment awareness embodied. Perhaps we can all take a little something from The Dude, and simply “abide.”
