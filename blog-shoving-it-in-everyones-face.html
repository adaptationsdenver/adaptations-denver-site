Useful Nonsense Part 4: Shoving It in Everyone’s Face

On values, and why a direction beats a destination.

November 15, 2026 · Brian Mulligan, LPCC, CAT

As I’m writing this, Thanksgiving 2026 is just around the corner. I’m looking out my kitchen window, watching the trees get more naked by the day. They are too suggestive, and I tell them to cover up.

Lewdness of the local foliage notwithstanding, fall is a beautiful time of year. The weather turns cold and everything dies. The days get shorter, the green becomes brown, the plants shrivel up, and people seek refuge indoors. This, somehow, is when most of us start to feel the “holiday spirit.”

How does a season that should inspire nothing but despair contain so much warmth? What is it about a natural process this objectively bleak that brings out compassion, kinship, and love? My theory is simple: when things get dark, we’re reminded of what’s actually important.

Which brings us, faithful reader, to values.

All Too Soft and Fuzzy

It’s hard to talk about values without sounding corny, or like a new-age huckster buttering you up before the pitch for locally-sourced artisanal kombucha and raw milk. The word arrives pre-laminated. It lives on the wall of a corporate lobby, next to a stock photo of a rowing team.

So let’s use a better example: a man nobody has ever accused of being soft.

Nice Game, From a Not-So-Nice Guy

Almost no one on Earth carries as much lore as Michael Jordan. The flu game. The gambling rumors. The Last Dance confirmed some of the myths and left others standing. What nobody disputes is the bloodthirsty competitiveness.

In March of 1993, a Washington Bullets guard named LaBradford Smith had the night of his life against the Bulls and dropped 37 points. Afterward, Jordan told his teammates that Smith had thrown an arm around him on the way off the floor and said, “Nice game, Mike.”

The two teams played again the very next night. Jordan had 36 by halftime.

Years later, he admitted that Smith never said it. He made the whole thing up.

The greatest basketball player alive invented an insult so that he could go avenge it. That is useful nonsense in its purest form, and it tells you exactly what MJ cared about most: shoving it in everyone’s face. In other words: what Michael Jordan valued, above all else, was conquering challenges and proving people wrong.

The greatest basketball player alive invented an insult so that he could go avenge it. That is useful nonsense in its purest form.

So, values need no halo. They require no sheen of “do-goodery.” They are not equivalent with religious platitudes or the “Golden Rule.” Mother Teresa had hers and Jordan had his. In Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, a value is a quality of action that is entirely up to you: how you want to behave, on an ongoing basis, in the parts of life that matter to you personally. Yours might be competing, building, protecting, telling the truth, or being the guy who shows up. Nobody gets to decide what your values are – or even what constitutes a value – except for you.

Values need no halo… Mother Teresa had hers and Jordan had his.

A Direction Beats a Destination

A goal is a destination. You get there, you cross it off, and it’s over. A value is a direction, like west. You can drive west for the rest of your life and never arrive at West.

Jordan’s goal was a championship. He won it, and the wanting was still there, so he went and won five more. The goals kept getting crossed off. The direction never ran out.

This is also the exit ramp from the arrival fallacy in Part 3. If the song is the point, your values are what you’re listening for.

Eating Your Vegetables

Nobody wants to run wind sprints. Nobody wants the hard conversation, the 5 a.m. alarm, or the first therapy appointment. Motivation is a feeling, and you can’t schedule a feeling. Your puppet master, meanwhile, has a prepared statement on why today is a bad day for it.

Values give you a reason that still works when the feeling is gone. You do not feel like driving to your kid’s 6 a.m. hockey practice in January. You go because being that kind of father matters more to you than a warm bed. And your kids don’t feel like eating their vegetables – but, when you tell them their favorite athletes do, they are a little more likely to try those brussels sprouts. Because they value getting better in athletics.

Motivation is a feeling, and you can’t schedule a feeling.

The discomfort costs the same either way. What changes is what it buys.

Jordan’s invented slight didn’t make game 2 any less “hard” in objective, physical measurement. It gave the effort somewhere to go.

This is why ACT bothers with acceptance and defusion in the first place. Making room for a painful feeling (Part 2) is a strange hobby unless it’s in service of something. Values are that something.

Feeling Good vs. Feeling Good About Yourself

These are two different projects, and they often point in opposite directions.

Feeling good is a full-time job with terrible management. The third drink, the scroll, and the skipped workout all feel good for about eleven minutes. There’s a word for arranging your life around this: hedonism – from the old Greek hēdonē, which literally translates to “pleasure.” Scientists sometimes refer to the brain mechanisms that promote short-term pleasure-seeking as the “dopaminergic cascade.” This is all a fancy way of saying you’re chasing the next pleasant sensation like a dog after a tennis ball.

Feeling good about yourself is built from evidence. A sense of self that lives only in your head is whatever story the puppet master happens to be telling that day, and he is constantly revising the script. A sense of self built on what you did in the real world this week is much harder to argue with. You showed up. You said the true thing. You went to the practice.

Ordinary moments get “heavier” this way, in the good sense. A Tuesday spent moving toward something you care about carries more weight than a Saturday spent numbing out. There’s more meat on the bone. When people invest in values, there’s no guarantee they’ll be rewarded with the same sense of pleasure, but they often find more of what’s been missing in their lives – a sense of fulfillment.

The Dumb Move

Your smart brain wants to do this properly. It wants a ranked list, a framework, and certainty that you’ve identified the correct values before you act on a single one. That project can run for years.

The dumb move is to skip the soul-searching and check the receipts. Think of the last miserable thing you did on purpose. You sat through the recital. You drove four hours to a funeral. You finished the thing you said you’d finish. Now ask what it was for. Whatever you just answered is a value, already installed and running.

Then pick the thing you’re dreading this week and say out loud, in one sentence, what it’s for. It will sound dumb. Jordan’s sentence was a lie and it worked fine. Yours has the advantage of being true.

The Why and the How

Viktor Frankl was a Viennese psychiatrist who spent three years in Nazi concentration camps, Auschwitz among them. He lost his wife, his parents, and his brother. In Man’s Search for Meaning, he keeps returning to a line from Nietzsche: “He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how.” He watched it play out around him. The prisoners who held onto a reason, a person to see again or a piece of work to finish, were the ones more likely to endure.

It is unlikely your Tuesday is comparable to a concentration camp. But if meaning could bear weight there, it’s a pretty good barometer for what it can bear in the day-to-day “trenches” of contemporary American life.

We can’t get rid of suffering. This whole series has been one long list of things you can’t do: you can’t fire the puppet master, you can’t make the floor foam, and you can’t skip the song. Here’s the one thing you can do: you can make it worth it.

’Tis the Season (For Whatever Matters to You)

The trees continue to undress outside my window. It’s dark before five. Next week most of us will drive farther than we’d like to sit at a crowded table and eat dry turkey with people we love. Somebody will bring up politics, and the whole table will brace hoping that Uncle Jeffrey doesn’t say something racist. Yet, when we look back at these gatherings, we can appreciate why we went. As the sun starts to hide and the days get dark, what matters most gets easier to see.
